# What the buyer can verify from one leaf

The owner signs a commitment to **one atomic batch**. A witness must see every move, verify all input controllers and policy, reject duplicate parents, reserve all inputs in a single durable transaction and retain the full signed proposal before releasing a vote. A collector combines q signatures. No trusted central batch builder is necessary; the controller or an untrusted relay can assemble the list. Withholding proposals, votes or paths remains a denial of service.

The codec uses SHA-256, distinct leaf/node/pad/statement/role tags, 32-byte keys and version IDs, big-endian u32 counts and indices. Sort by unique parent. Hash leaf(index,parent,recipient), pad to the next power of two with deterministic indexed pad hashes, hash ordered pairs, then bind root, width, mode and authenticated domain into the statement. Width 1 has a zero-length path. A recipient verifies its expected parent, recipient, owner authorisation, exact index/path length, computed root, roster, q distinct signatures and lineage. Verification yields the same child for every valid signer subset. An unexpected recipient or different entitlement cannot discharge the buyer's expected purchase. The zome derives the resulting owner for a history; the buyer must compare that result with its own key.

**Security layers.** Collision resistance proves inclusion relative to the signed root. It does not prove that siblings are valid, disjoint, correctly ordered or available. A buyer accepting the compact witness certificate relies on at least one honest voter having checked the entire batch and on quorum intersection across batches. The full-audit Phase 1 verifier could recompute every sibling's validity with its ancestry. Compact acceptance removes that audit capability from the leaf alone. If independently re-executing all sibling semantics without trusting honest validation is a requirement, provide the full dependencies or a sound validity proof over an authenticated state commitment. A SNARK proving validity against one of two conflicting state roots does not establish unique finality.

**Availability.** Every honest voting process stores the complete body with its reservation before signing. A receiving buyer needs its own proof and ancestry. Other recipients may still be missing theirs after the entire batch is already final. With q votes and at most e faulty identities, at least q−e non-faulty voters held the body at release under the durable-storage assumption. To reconstruct paths later, reach a retaining signer, retrieve and hash-check the body; to reconstruct a *lost certificate* one also needs q usable signatures. A client can lose a certificate if Byzantine voters refuse to repeat their signatures. Body availability alone does not imply certificate availability, fair delivery, or durable archival after retirement. The implementation tests fetching and checking proofs after a collector's simulated loss. A non-signer can correctly return “missing body”.

**Costs.** For m leaves one path adds 32 ceil(log2 m) bytes, apart from leaf, metadata, owner and quorum signatures. A full list costs 64m raw bytes for pairs. For 64 leaves, six sibling hashes replace 63 other pairs. The signer still reads O(m) data and checks all inputs. Merkle construction is O(m); if serving many recipients cache levels once. The laboratory builds a tree for each requested receipt. Small batches can be cheaper in flat form. Repeatedly sending the same quorum proof to many buyers is not a reduction in independent signers. See [measured serialised receipt sizes](network-benchmarks.md). [Merkle inclusion proofs and domain separation precedent](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9162)
